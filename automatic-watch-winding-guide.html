Automatic Watch Winding

Automatic watches wind themselves as you move, but only while you wear them. Here is how to start one that has stopped, set it safely and keep it healthy.

How it winds

A weighted rotor inside spins as your wrist moves and winds the mainspring. Off the wrist, the watch runs on its stored power reserve, usually 38–80 hours, then stops.

Starting a stopped watch

With the crown in its first position (pushed in, or unscrewed for screw-down crowns), turn it clockwise 20–40 times. Most automatics have no hard stop, and it will not harm the watch. Then set the time.

Setting the date safely

On many watches, changing the date between about 9 pm and 3 am can strain the date mechanism while it is already moving. Set the hands to around 6 o’clock first, adjust the date, then set the correct time.

Let it stop

It is perfectly fine for an automatic to stop between wears. It does not damage the movement, and many owners prefer to simply wind and set it when they next pick it up.

Winders

A winder keeps a watch running by turning it gently. Use the maker’s recommended turns per day and direction; too many turns only add wear. They are most useful for complicated calendars that are slow to set.

Service

Plan a service every five to eight years, or sooner if it gains or loses noticeably, stops early or the crown feels gritty. Have water resistance checked at the same time.

“Wind it, set it, wear it. An automatic is simpler than it looks.”
